# Goal
Turn any study session into recall practice: close the source, pull the ideas out of your own head, check, and correct. Write questions that make you explain and use an idea, and rate flashcards honestly.

# Why
The last lesson showed that rereading builds a feeling of knowing that doesn't last. This lesson gives you the replacement. Recalling an idea doesn't just measure what you know: the act of recalling makes the memory stronger and easier to find next time. It's the single most useful study habit in this course, and it's why every lesson in 4YOS ends with a check and why passed checks become flashcards.

# Warm-up
?? What's the difference between recognising an idea and recalling it?
=> Recognising means you know it when you see it. Recalling means you can produce it with the source out of sight.

?? Why keep your first score, even after you've checked and corrected it?
=> It's the honest record of what you produced from memory. A corrected score hides the gap.

# Lesson
## The four-step cycle
**Retrieval practice** means bringing information back to mind with the source closed. It works as a way of learning, not only as a test. Each attempt follows the same cycle.

1. **Close** the book, the notes or the video.
2. **Recall**: write or say everything you can, or answer a question.
3. **Check** against the source.
4. **Correct**: write down only what you missed or got wrong, then try that part again with the source closed.

Do it soon. You don't need to feel ready, and waiting until you do turns the session back into rereading. Try a first recall straight after your first read of a section.

Getting things wrong during practice is useful, as long as you check afterwards. A wrong answer that you then correct tends to be remembered better than an answer you were simply shown. What matters is the check. Practice without checking can strengthen a mistake.

### What counts as recall
| Recall | Not recall |
| --- | --- |
| Writing a definition from memory, then checking it | Reading the definition again |
| Explaining a method to an empty chair | Watching the method again |
| Working a fresh problem with notes closed | Copying a worked example neatly |
| A blank page: "everything I know about binary" | Highlighting the key lines |

## Questions that make you think
The question you ask yourself decides what you practise. A question that asks you to recognise a phrase trains recognition. A question that asks you to explain or use an idea trains the skill an exam and a job will ask for.

| Weak question | Stronger question |
| --- | --- |
| What does RAM stand for? | What happens to what's in RAM when the power goes, and why does that matter for unsaved work? |
| Is a byte 8 bits? | How many values can one byte hold, and why that many? |
| Define the mean. | When would the mean mislead you about a set of incomes, and what would you use instead? |

Good recall questions usually ask **why**, **how**, **what if** or **what's the difference**. Write them as you read: turn each heading into a question, and answer it once the page is closed.

### The blank page
When a topic is large, start with a **brain dump**. Take a blank page, set three minutes, and write everything you can about the topic, in any order. Then open the source and mark three things: what you got right, what you missed, and what you got wrong. The missed and wrong parts are your study list for the next session.

> Mistake: Making beautiful notes and calling that recall. Copying and arranging ideas while looking at them feels like hard work, but you're not producing anything from memory. Make the notes, then close them and recall.

## Flashcards, honestly
Flashcards are recall practice in small pieces, and 4YOS makes them for you from each passed check. They only work if you treat each card as a real attempt.

1. Read the front, and **say or write your answer before you turn it over**.
2. Turn it over and compare.
3. Rate what really happened.

The rating is what decides when the card comes back. 4YOS uses a scheduler called FSRS. It brings a card back sooner when you struggled and later when it was easy. If you rate a card "Good" because the answer looked familiar once you saw it, the scheduler treats it as known. An inflated rating can delay the next review beyond what suits your real recall.

| What happened | Honest rating |
| --- | --- |
| Blank, or wrong | Again |
| Got it, but slowly or with a struggle | Hard |
| Got it with normal effort | Good |
| Instant and certain | Easy |

> Mistake: Flipping the card before giving a real answer, then thinking "I knew that". If you didn't produce it, it's Again.

## Making it a habit
Recall needs a fixed place in your routine, or rereading quietly takes over again.

- **After a lecture or reading:** spend 10 minutes that same day writing what you remember, then check.
- **At the start of a session:** before new work, recall the last session's main points from memory.
- **Before an exam:** spend most of your time on questions with notes closed, and the least on rereading.

Recall can feel harder than rereading. Some difficulty is normal, and researchers call helpful effort a **desirable difficulty**, but difficulty alone doesn't prove anything is sticking. Judge by results: can you recall it correctly on a later check? If you keep drawing a blank, use a cue or review the answer, then try again.

# Exercises
?? Rewrite this weak question: "What does CPU stand for?"
=> For example: "What does the processor do that RAM doesn't, and what happens to each when the power goes?" It asks you to explain and use the idea.

?? You answer a flashcard wrong, then see the answer and think it's obvious. How should you rate it?
=> Again. You didn't produce it, and an honest rating brings it back soon enough to learn it.

?? You did a brain dump on a topic and got half of it. What do you do next?
=> Check against the source, write down only what you missed or got wrong, and retry those parts with the source closed.

# Practice
Use one real topic you're studying now, or an earlier lesson here.

1. Read one short section once, then close it.
2. Do a three-minute brain dump, with no peeking.
3. Open the source and mark what was right, missed or wrong.
4. Write three recall questions about the section, each starting with why, how, what if, or what's the difference.
5. Answer them tomorrow with the source closed, then check.

Write down the topic, what you missed on the first attempt, your three questions, and how you did the next day.

# Rubric
- I recalled from memory with the source closed before checking.
- I wrote down what I missed or got wrong, not just what I got right.
- My questions ask me to explain or use the idea, and I answered them again the next day.

# Summary
- Recall with the source closed, check, then correct: the four-step cycle.
- Recalling strengthens memory. It's a way of learning, not just a test.
- Ask why, how, what if and what's the difference, not "what does it stand for".
- Answer a flashcard before you turn it, and rate what really happened.
- Recall should feel hard. That effort is a sign it's working.

Q: Why must you check after a recall attempt?
P: The four-step cycle
- Because checking is what puts the information into memory in the first place
* Without checking, you may strengthen a mistake
- Because recall attempts don't count unless someone marks them
= The check catches errors. Practising a wrong answer makes the wrong answer stronger.

P: Questions that make you think
- What does RAM stand for?
- Is RAM a type of computer memory?
* Why is unsaved work lost when the power goes?

Q: What is a brain dump?
P: Questions that make you think
- Reading every page of your notes very quickly from start to finish
* Writing all you can recall about a topic, then checking it
- Copying the main points of a chapter onto a single page
= It's recall on a blank page, followed by marking right, missed and wrong.

Q: You turn a flashcard over before answering, and the answer looks familiar. How should you rate it?
P: Flashcards, honestly
- Good, because you clearly recognised the answer
- Easy, because the answer looked obvious once you saw it
* Again, because you didn't produce an answer
= Recognising isn't recalling. Without a real attempt, it's a miss.

Q: What happens if you rate cards too generously?
P: Flashcards, honestly
* The next review may come too late for your real recall
- Nothing changes, because ratings don't affect when cards return
- The cards come back sooner, so you just review them more often
= Your rating, together with the card's history and the time since you last saw it, sets the next review.

Q: Recall feels much harder than rereading. What should you conclude?
P: Making it a habit
- The method isn't working, so you should go back to rereading for good
* Check your answer, and try again after a delay
- You've learned as much as you can possibly remember for today
= Judge by whether you can recall it correctly later, not by how hard it felt.

Q[transfer]: You follow a programming tutorial video and it all makes sense, but you can't write the code alone. What should you change?

- Watch the same video again at a slower speed
- Find a video with clearer pictures and animations
* Close the video and write it from a blank file, then compare
= Following along builds familiarity. Producing the code yourself is the skill you need.

= It's the four-step cycle, used on the real kind of question.

Q[transfer]: In a course reading, every heading is a statement. How can you turn the reading into recall practice?
