# Project
Build your own study system, and use it for two real weeks with your own courses. Then report what happened, with real numbers. Keep private details out: no names of other people, no passwords, and no copied course material.

Your report needs these five parts.

1. **Your plan.** Your weekly plan for both weeks: fixed commitments from official sources, about 80% of free time planned, spaced sessions, same-day recall after lectures, at least one mixed practice session a week, and sleep as a fixed commitment.
2. **Your methods in use.** For one topic at each school, show your recall attempts, with predictions and first scores kept honest, your review dates and how each review went.
3. **Your notes.** One page of notes in your own words with cue questions and a summary, and how many cues you answered from memory a few days later.
4. **Your weekly reviews.** Both reviews: what you planned against what you did, what slipped and why, and the one change you made for the next week.
5. **What you'll keep.** Which methods you'll keep using, which you'll change, and why, judged by your own results, not by how each method felt.

Write it in your own words. Your own record is the point, so don't have an AI write it. This project is self-checked, and the app will record it that way.

# Project rubric
- All five parts are present, with real numbers from two real weeks.
- My recall scores and predictions are recorded honestly, with first scores kept separate from corrections.
- I did both weekly reviews and changed one thing each week.
- I judged methods by my results, not by how they felt.
- It contains no private details or copied course material.

# Exam
Q: You reread a chapter and it feels familiar. What does that feeling show by itself?
- That you can explain the chapter with the book closed
* That you recognise it when you see it
- That you'll remember it in a week
= Familiarity needs the cues in front of you. Recall is tested with them gone.

Q: You predicted four points and wrote three before checking. What do you record?
- Four, since the last point was obvious once you looked
* A prediction of four and a first score of three
- Three for the prediction and three for the score
= Keep the prediction and first score honest, and record corrections separately.

Q: Put the recall cycle in order.
O: Close the source | Recall | Check | Correct what you missed
= Recall first, then check against the source, then fix the gaps.

Q: Which question best trains understanding?
- What does RAM stand for?
- Is a byte eight bits?
* Why does the median resist outliers?
= Why-questions make you explain, not just recognise.

Q: You didn't produce a flashcard's answer, but it looks obvious now. How should you rate it?
- Good
- Easy
* Again
= Recognising isn't recalling. An honest rating sets the right schedule.

Q: Which plan uses spacing for 3 hours of study before a test in a week?
- 3 hours the night before the test
* About 45 minutes on four different days
- 3 hours of rereading the morning of the test
= The same time spread across days lasts much longer.

Q: What does a gap between study sessions let you do?
- Clear space in your memory for brand new topics
* Test what you can really recall after some time has passed
- Avoid ever needing to review the topic again
= A later attempt shows what lasted, and what needs another look.

Q: A review went badly. What should happen to the next gap?
* It should be shorter
- It should be longer
- It should stay exactly the same
= Hard reviews mean come back sooner.

Q: What does interleaving train that blocked practice doesn't?
- Doing one method's steps faster
* Choosing which method fits each problem
- Remembering the textbook's exact wording
= In a mixed set, you must decide the method every time.

Q: When is a short block of one problem type useful?
* When the method is completely new
- When revising for an exam next week
- When you already know the method well
= Learn the steps first, then mix.

Q: What is self-explanation?
- Reading a worked example silently, from start to end
* Saying what each step does and why it's allowed
- Copying a worked example until it's memorised
= Explaining each step shows exactly where understanding has holes.

Q: Why use two concrete examples that look different?
- Exams always ask for exactly two examples
* To see the idea itself, not one surface detail
- Two examples make your notes longer and more complete
= Different surfaces reveal what the examples share.

Q: What goes into a weekly plan first?
- Study sessions for your favourite course
* Fixed commitments from official sources
- Empty buffer time for surprises
= Real free time only shows once the fixed things are in.

Q: Which plan leaves room for interruptions?
- Every free hour booked, so that no time is wasted
* Most free hours planned, with some left empty as a buffer
- No plan at all, deciding each evening what to do
= The buffer absorbs the unexpected, so the plan survives.

Q: You planned 10 hours and did 6. Which adjustment uses what you learned?
- 10 hours again, but trying harder this time
* Plan around what you really managed, then adjust after the next review
- 14 hours, to catch up on everything missed
= Plan for your demonstrated capacity, then grow it.

Q: What is generative note-taking?
- Writing down everything, word for word
* Putting ideas into your own words, choosing what matters
- Photographing each slide to read later
= Rewording and choosing force you to think about meaning.

Q: In Cornell notes, what goes in the left margin?
* Questions about the notes
- A copy of the slides
- The date and course code
= The cue questions turn the page into recall practice.

Q: In a self-paced course, what should you read first?
- The last unit
* The course guide
- Last year's exam
= The guide says what's assessed, every deadline and how many units there are.

Q: Where should deadlines come from?
- What classmates remember
* The official portal and notices
- Last year's dates for the same course
= Details change, so check official sources.

Q: When should you ask for help with a course?
- In the last week, when you know all your gaps
* Early, as soon as you're stuck
- Never, as you must manage alone
= Early questions are easy to answer.

Q: Which source decides how many questions to answer in an exam?
- The last past paper you practised
* The current official instruction
- The fullest model answer you've found
= Past formats can change. Follow the latest verified rule.

Q: A 60-minute mock has 10 minutes for reading and checking, and 50 marks. What's a starting budget for a 15-mark question?
- 10 minutes
* 15 minutes
- 18 minutes
= 50 writing minutes for 50 marks is one minute per mark.

Q: Why is an all-night cram before an exam a poor trade?
- Exams are usually in the afternoon
* You lose the sleep that strengthens memory, and think less clearly
- Extra hours never help
= A few tired hours cost consolidation and clear thinking.

Q: Which is a real break between study blocks?
- Ten minutes of scrolling
* Standing up, moving and drinking water
- Starting a new topic straight away
= Scrolling keeps attention busy. Moving rests it.

Q[transfer]: You must learn a new programming language for a job in two months. Which plan fits this course?
- Twelve hours over one weekend just before starting
* A little most days, recalling what you did before, with mixed small programs
- Reading the whole manual once, now
= Spaced, recall-based, mixed practice lasts.

Q[transfer]: A friend's practice scores are excellent, but their exam scores are poor. Each session is one topic, open book. What would you suggest?
- Longer sessions on each topic
* Mixed topics, notes closed, and naming the method before solving
- Rereading the textbook instead of practising
= Open-book blocked practice inflates scores. Closed, mixed practice trains the exam's skill.

Q[transfer]: At work, a manager asks you to learn a new system in a month with no training. What should you do first?
* Set weekly targets, test yourself as you go, and ask early when stuck
- Wait until you need it, then learn it in one go
- Read the manual once and hope it sticks
= Self-regulation works for any self-paced learning.

Q[transfer]: You're learning to spot phishing, malware and weak passwords. How should you practise?
- One threat a week, in order
* Mixed real scenarios, deciding which threat each one shows
- One long article covering all three
= Deciding which threat fits is the real-life skill, so practise it mixed.

Q[transfer]: A classmate offers you their finished assignment "to check your answers". What should you do?
- Copy the parts you're unsure of
* Decline and discuss the topic instead, since submitted work must be your own
- Submit it, since it's a small assignment
= Studying together is fine. Submitting someone else's work isn't.

Q[transfer]: You've had poor sleep and low mood for several weeks, and study feels impossible. What's the right step?
- Wait until after the exams
* Speak to a health professional, and tell someone you trust
- Study more to take your mind off it
= Persistent low mood and poor sleep deserve real help.
