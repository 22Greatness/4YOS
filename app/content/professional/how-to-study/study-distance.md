# Goal
Run your own learning when there's no lecturer setting the pace: read the course guide, set your own deadlines, check your own progress, use other people, and ask for help early.

# Why
In a distance or self-paced course, much of the teaching is in the course materials, and there may be few required sessions. That freedom is useful for someone doing two degrees, and it's also the main reason distance learners fall behind: without a timetable, study slips quietly until a deadline arrives. The skills in this lesson are the ones a self-paced learner needs, and they're the same ones you'll need for self-study at work later.

# Lesson
## You set the pace now
In a face-to-face course, the lecturer decides what to cover each week, and the lectures remind you. In a distance course, the materials are the teacher and you're the timetable. The ability to plan, track and adjust your own learning is called **self-regulation**. It has three parts, and you've met pieces of each already.

| Part | What it means | Tools from this course |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plan | Set goals and when you'll work on them | The weekly plan |
| Monitor | Check whether you're really learning | Recall, honest scores, calibration |
| Adjust | Change what isn't working | The weekly review |

The most common failure isn't lack of ability. It's that study without a fixed time gets pushed back, again and again, until it's too late to do well.

## Read the course guide first
Start with the course guide or the official course page, if there is one. Check which sessions and deadlines are required. The guide is the closest thing you have to a lecturer explaining the course on the first day. Read it before anything else, and write down:

1. **What's assessed**, and how marks are split between assignments and the exam.
2. **Every deadline**, such as tutor-marked assignments, into your plan right away.
3. **The units**, and how many there are, so you can spread them out.
4. **How the exam works**, and what you're allowed to use.

Check these against the official portal and notices, since details can change between semesters. Don't rely on what you remember from another course.

### Make your own deadlines
With the units counted and the exam date known, give each unit its own target date, working backwards from the exam. Leave the last two or three weeks for mixed revision and past questions. Then put the unit sessions in your weekly plan as fixed commitments, as if they were lectures.

| Exam in 12 weeks, 18 units | Plan |
| --- | --- |
| Weeks 1 to 9 | 2 units a week, with recall the next day |
| Weeks 10 to 12 | Mixed revision, past questions, weak topics |

> Mistake: "I'll do the self-paced course when I have a free weekend." Free weekends rarely come, and one long weekend is cramming anyway. Give it fixed weekly sessions.

## Learning with other people
Studying alone doesn't have to mean studying lonely. Other people help you stay on pace and help you learn.

- **A study partner or small group** can meet, in person or online, to explain topics to each other and test each other. Explaining to someone else is self-explanation with a listener. Agree on a fixed time, and set a topic for each meeting.
- **Facilitators and study centres** exist to help. Find out what yours offers, and when.
- **Course forums and class groups** are useful for news and questions, but check anything important against official sources.

A group works best when each person comes having already tried the material. A group that only shares answers to assignments doesn't help anyone learn, and it can break the school's rules on your own work. Assignments you submit must be your own.

## Checking yourself and asking early
Without a lecturer's questions in class, you need other ways to find out how you're doing.

- Use the **self-assessment questions** at the end of each unit, with the material closed, and score yourself honestly.
- Keep a short **progress log**: unit, date, recall score. A falling score tells you to slow down or revisit.
- Do **past questions** from each school's authorised sources well before the exam.

When you're stuck, **ask early**. A question asked in week 3 is easy to answer. Ten unanswered questions in week 11 become a crisis. Write a clear question: what you're studying, what you've tried, and exactly where you're stuck. Then ask a facilitator, a study partner, or Ayo inside the lesson.

> Note: Ayo in 4YOS can explain and quiz you, but it doesn't know your school's rules or deadlines. Those always come from the school itself.

# Exercises
?? A course has 15 units and the exam is in 10 weeks. Make a rough plan.
=> For example: 2 units a week for weeks 1 to 8, leaving weeks 9 and 10 for mixed revision and past questions.

?? Your recall scores on a course have fallen for three units in a row. What should you do?
=> Slow down, revisit the earlier units with recall, and ask about anything that's still unclear.

?? Write a clear question to a facilitator about something you're stuck on.
=> For example: "In Unit 4, I don't follow why the median resists outliers. I tried it with my own numbers, but I'm unsure. Could you explain with an example?"

# Practice
Use one real course where you study mostly alone.

1. Read its course guide, and write down what's assessed, every deadline and the number of units. Check them against the official portal.
2. Work backwards from the exam: give each unit a target week, and leave the last weeks for revision.
3. Put the unit sessions into your weekly plan as fixed commitments.
4. Start a progress log, and record your recall score after each unit.
5. Find one person or group to study with, or find out what your study centre offers.

Write your units, your target weeks and your first two log entries. Keep private details out.

# Rubric
- I read the real course guide and checked the deadlines officially.
- I set my own target dates for each unit, with revision time at the end.
- I keep a progress log and have a plan for getting help.

# Summary
- In a distance course, the materials teach and you set the pace.
- Self-regulation: plan, monitor and adjust.
- Read the course guide first, and check details against official sources.
- Set your own unit deadlines, working back from the exam.
- Study with others, keep your submitted work your own, and ask for help early.

Q[transfer]: A classmate offers you their finished assignment "to check your answers". What should you do?

- Submit it as your own, since it's only a small assignment
= Studying together is fine. Submitting someone else's work isn't.

- The log should record hours studied, not scores
= Open-book scores hide gaps. Test with the material closed.
