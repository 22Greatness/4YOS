# Goal
Build a real weekly plan for two schools: start from what's fixed, fit spaced recall sessions into the gaps, keep space for the unexpected, and check the plan against what really happened.

# Why
Two degrees at once means two sets of lectures, readings, assignments and exams, with deadlines that don't know about each other. Without a plan, the loudest deadline wins every week, and spaced study never happens. A simple weekly plan turns the methods from the last lessons into actual hours. 4YOS's Today page does part of this for you, but the plan is still yours.

# Warm-up
?? Why spread three hours of study across several days instead of one evening?
=> Spaced study lasts much longer, because each return after a gap is real recall work.

?? Why mix problem types in a practice session?
=> To practise choosing the method, as an exam makes you do.

# Lesson
## Start with what's fixed
A weekly plan starts with things that can't move. These are **fixed commitments**, and you should record them from sources you can check, not memory.

| Kind | Examples | Where to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Classes | Lectures and practicals | Your faculty timetable |
| Deadlines | Assignments and coursework | Each course's guide or portal |
| Exams | Test and exam dates | Official notices from each school |
| Life | Work, travel, family duties, worship, sleep | You |

Put these in first. Then you can see the real free time, rather than the time you wish you had. Sleep is a fixed commitment too, as you'll see in the last lesson. A plan that only works if you sleep five hours isn't a plan.

> Note: Dates change. When a school changes a date, update the plan from the official notice, not from what someone said in a group chat. 4YOS's school calendar import keeps these in one place.

## Fit the study into the gaps
With the fixed items in place, decide how many hours of study you really have. Be honest: a free evening after a long day of lectures isn't three hours of good study.

Then give each course its share, using what you know works.

1. **Short and spaced beats long and rare.** Four 45-minute sessions beat one 3-hour session.
2. **Recall within a day of each lecture.** Book 10 to 20 minutes the same evening.
3. **Mix courses within a day.** Two different subjects in one evening is interleaving and keeps you fresher.
4. **Hard work in your best hours.** Put the most demanding work when you concentrate best, and easy tasks such as organising notes in tired hours.

### Worked example: one weekday
| Time | What | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 08:00–12:00 | Lectures | Fixed |
| 13:00–13:20 | Recall: this morning's statistics lecture | Same-day recall |
| 16:00–16:45 | A self-study course: one unit section | New learning |
| 16:50–17:20 | Mixed maths practice | Interleaving and spacing |
| 20:00–20:20 | Flashcards in 4YOS | Spaced review |

That's under two hours of study, in four pieces, around fixed classes. A day like this, repeated, does more than one heroic Saturday.

## Keep a buffer
Things go wrong. A lecture moves, power goes off, you get ill, a deadline appears. If every hour of your week is booked, one surprise knocks everything over, and you abandon the plan.

So leave part of your free time empty as a **buffer**. Planning about **80%** is a reasonable starting point, which you adjust after a week or two to suit how your weeks really go. When something goes wrong, the buffer absorbs it. When nothing does, use it to get ahead or rest.

### Big tasks into small pieces
An assignment or a project due in three weeks isn't one task. Split it into pieces of under an hour, and spread them across the weeks: read the question and plan, find sources, write a first section, finish the draft, check and submit. Each piece is small enough to start on a tired evening, and starting is often the hardest part.

> Mistake: Writing "work on assignment" in the plan. It's too vague to start. Write the next concrete piece, such as "write the introduction, 300 words".

## Check the plan against reality
At the end of each week, take 15 minutes to compare the plan with what really happened. This is the weekly review, and 4YOS has a place for it in Goals.

| Question | Why ask it |
| --- | --- |
| What did I actually do? | The honest record, not the intended one |
| What got skipped, and why? | Too ambitious, wrong time of day, or a real emergency |
| Which deadlines are in the next two weeks? | So nothing arrives as a surprise |
| What one change will I make next week? | One change, not a whole new system |

If you planned 10 hours and did 6, plan a little above what you managed, such as 7, rather than 10 again. A plan you keep beats a plan you admire. Over a few weeks, your plans will match your real capacity.

### Two schools in one plan
When both schools need the same week, decide by what's due first and what counts most. If a topic appears in both, learn it once and practise each school's own questions. And protect the courses with no lectures to remind you, usually the self-study ones: without a fixed session in the plan, they're the first to slip.

# Exercises
?? You have 8 free hours this week. How many should you plan?
=> About 6 or 7, leaving the rest as a buffer.

?? Rewrite "study for exams" as a plan item you could start on a tired evening.
=> For example: "Do 5 past questions on percentages, notes closed, 30 minutes".

?? You planned 12 hours last week and managed 7. What should you do?
=> Plan about 8 next week, and note why the rest was skipped.

# Practice
Plan your real coming week.

1. List every fixed commitment, from official timetables and course guides where you can.
2. Count your real free hours, and plan only part of them, about 80% to start, leaving a buffer.
3. Fit in spaced sessions for each course, with same-day recall after lectures and at least one mixed practice session.
4. Split any assignment due within three weeks into pieces of under an hour.
5. At the end of the week, do the four-question review, and write one change for next week.

Write your planned hours, your actual hours and your one change. Keep private details out.

# Rubric
- My plan starts from real fixed commitments, checked against official sources.
- I planned about 80% of my free time, with spaced and mixed sessions.
- I reviewed the week honestly and chose one change.

# Summary
- Put fixed commitments in first, from sources you can check.
- Short, spaced sessions, with recall the same day as each lecture.
- Plan about 80% of free time, and keep the rest as a buffer.
- Split big tasks into concrete pieces of under an hour.
- Review each week: what happened, what slipped, what's coming, one change.

Q: Which plan is better for one course this week?

* Four 45-minute sessions on different days
- One 3-hour session on Saturday afternoon
- Whatever time is left after everything else
= Short, spaced sessions are what the earlier lessons showed works.

Q: When should you recall a lecture?
P: Fit the study into the gaps
- Only in the week before the exam
* Within a day, ideally the same evening
- Never, because attending the lecture is enough
= Same-day recall starts the spacing and catches gaps early.

Q: Why plan only about 80% of your free time?
P: Keep a buffer
- Because study only works for 80% of every hour
* So a surprise doesn't knock the whole plan over
- Because 20% of the time should always be for sleep
= The buffer absorbs the unexpected.

Q: Which plan item is easiest to start on a tired evening?
P: Keep a buffer
- Work on the assignment
- Study statistics
* Write 300 words of the introduction
= A concrete piece is easy to start. Vague items get put off.

Q: You planned 10 hours and did 6. What should next week's plan be?
P: Check the plan against reality
- 10 hours again, but try harder this time
* About 7 hours, then adjust from there
- 14 hours, to catch up on what was missed
= Plan for your real capacity, then grow it.

Q: Which course is most likely to slip?
P: Check the plan against reality
- The one with the most lectures each week
* The one with no lectures to remind you, unless it has a fixed session
- The one with the earliest exam date
= Self-study courses need their own fixed sessions in the plan.

Q[transfer]: You've got a job interview prep, an assignment and a class test all within ten days. What should you do first?
P: Keep a buffer
* List the deadlines, split each into small pieces, and spread them across the days
- Start with whichever one feels most urgent today, and do it all at once
- Wait until each deadline is close, then focus on it alone
= Splitting and spreading lets all three move forward without a crash.

Q[transfer]: A friend keeps abandoning their plans by Wednesday. What's the most likely fix?
P: Keep a buffer
- Make the plans more detailed, with every hour of the day booked
* Plan less, leave a buffer, and review honestly each week
- Stop planning and study whenever they feel like it
= Overfull plans break at the first surprise.

Q[transfer]: The same topic appears in two of your courses this week. What's efficient?
P: Check the plan against reality
- Study it twice, separately, once for each course
* Learn it once, then practise each school's own questions
- Study it only for the school whose exam is first
= Learn once, then practise each school's style.
