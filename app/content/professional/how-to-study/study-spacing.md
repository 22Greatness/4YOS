# Goal
Spread study across days instead of cramming, plan when to come back to a topic, and explain why a little forgetting between sessions helps.

# Why
With two degrees and limited hours, when you study matters as much as how. The same total time, split across several days, leads to much better memory a week or a month later than one long session. Your courses build on each other for four years, so learning that lasts saves you relearning later. It's also why 4YOS brings flashcards and week-later checks back on a schedule.

# Lesson
## Cramming against spacing
**Massed practice**, or cramming, means studying a topic in one long block. **Spaced practice** means dividing the same amount of study into shorter sessions on different days.

| Plan | Total time | How it feels | A week later |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 hours the night before | 3 hours | Productive, the topic feels fresh | Much of it is gone |
| 45 minutes on four separate days | 3 hours | Harder, as you've forgotten some each time | Far more remains |

Cramming can get you through tomorrow's test. Research on spacing, across hundreds of experiments with words, facts, maths and skills, has repeatedly found that spaced study wins on later tests. The gap gets bigger the longer you need to remember.

## Why forgetting helps
This sounds wrong at first. If you forget some of a topic between sessions, why is that better?

A gap means that when you come back, you find out what you can really recall after some time has passed, instead of repeating something that's still fresh in your mind. In a single long session, the second and third passes through a topic happen while it's still in your head. They feel easy, and they add little. After a gap, each return is real recall work.

This is the same **desirable difficulty** you met with recall practice: what feels harder often works better. Spacing and recall work best together. When you come back after a gap, try recalling first. If you can't, review the answer and try again. Spaced restudy helps too, but recalling first shows you what has really lasted.

### The forgetting curve
Over a century ago, the psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus measured how quickly he forgot lists he'd learned. Memory falls quickly at first, then more slowly. Each time you successfully recall something, the fall afterwards is slower. That's the reason a review schedule spreads out: each successful review earns a longer gap before the next.

## Making a schedule
You don't need to calculate anything precise. A simple pattern works: review a new topic after about **1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 2 weeks and a month**, using recall each time. If a review goes badly, shorten the next gap. If it's easy, lengthen it.

How far apart should the first sessions be? It depends on how long you need to remember. Studies that varied the gap found that longer gaps suit longer goals. To remember for a week, reviews a day or two apart work well. To remember for months, gaps of a week or more are better. For an exam in six weeks, spread your sessions over all six weeks rather than the last few days.

### Let the app do the timing
When you pass a check in 4YOS, its main ideas become flashcards, and the FSRS scheduler sets each card's next review from your ratings. A card you find hard comes back sooner, and an easy one later. Your job is to show up for the review and rate honestly. The week-later check on each lesson is spacing too: it tests whether the lesson held after a gap.

## Spacing with two schools
Your timetable is fuller than most students'. Spacing makes it easier, not harder, because many short sessions fit in gaps that a long session can't.

| Situation | Spaced plan |
| --- | --- |
| A lecture on Monday | 10 minutes of recall Monday evening, again Wednesday, and again at the weekend |
| A unit you read alone | Read one section a day, and start each day by recalling yesterday's |
| An assignment due in three weeks | Start now, and do a small piece on several days, not one late night |
| An exam in six weeks | Cycle through every topic several times, with gaps, from now on |

> Mistake: Planning to "revise everything" in the last week. Spread the revision so each topic comes back several times. One pass per topic at the end is cramming in disguise.

# Exercises
?? You have 4 hours to prepare for a test in eight days. How should you use them?
=> Split them across several days, for example 1 hour on four different days, using recall rather than rereading.

?? A review was very hard, and you got most of it wrong. What should happen to the next gap?
=> Shorten it. Come back sooner, then lengthen the gaps again as it improves.

?? Why does rereading a topic after a gap waste the gap?
=> The gap makes recall effortful, and that effort is what strengthens memory. Rereading skips the effort.

# Practice
Use one real topic you've just learned at either school.

1. Do a recall session on it today, with the source closed, then check.
2. Put four review dates in your calendar: tomorrow, in 3 days, in a week and in 2 weeks.
3. At each review, recall first, then check. Write your score each time, out of the same number of points.
4. After the last review, look at your four scores. Did the topic hold better as the gaps grew?

Write your topic, your four dates and your four scores.

# Rubric
- I recalled the topic today and set real review dates in my calendar.
- I recalled first at every review, and recorded each score honestly.
- I compared my scores and wrote what they showed.

# Summary
- Spread study across days. The same time spaced out lasts far longer.
- A little forgetting makes the next recall harder, and that effort strengthens memory.
- Return after about 1 day, 3 days, a week, 2 weeks and a month, recalling each time.
- Longer goals need longer gaps.
- 4YOS times your flashcards from your ratings. Show up and rate honestly.

# Check
Q: What is spaced practice?
P: Cramming against spacing
- Studying one topic for a long session until it feels completely clear
* Dividing study time into shorter sessions on different days
- Leaving a gap of white space between the lines in your notes
= The same total time is spread out across days.

Q: You cram for 3 hours the night before a test. What's most likely?
P: Cramming against spacing
- You'll remember it better than with spaced study for many months
* It may help tomorrow, but much of it fades within a week
- You'll forget it all before the test begins the next morning
= Cramming can work for a test the next day. It does poorly for later.

Q: Why does some forgetting between sessions help?
P: Why forgetting helps
* Recalling a fading memory takes effort, and that strengthens it
- Forgetting clears space in your memory for completely new material
- It doesn't help at all, so you should avoid forgetting anything
= The effort of recall after a gap is the desirable difficulty.

Q: You come back to a topic after three days. What should you do first?
P: Why forgetting helps
- Reread the topic carefully, so that it's fresh again
* Try to recall it with the source closed, then check
- Skip it, since it'll still be there in your memory
= Recalling after a gap uses the gap. Rereading wastes it.

Q: What does the forgetting curve show?
P: Why forgetting helps
- Memory fades at exactly the same speed every day, forever
* Memory falls fast at first, then more slowly, and slower still after each review
- Memory gets stronger each day, even when you don't review
= It's why review gaps can grow after each successful recall.

Q: A review went badly. What should you do with the next gap?
P: Making a schedule
* Make it shorter
- Make it longer
- Keep it the same
= Hard reviews mean come back sooner. Easy ones earn a longer gap.

Q: You need to remember a topic for an exam in six weeks. How should you space it?
P: Making a schedule
- All the sessions in the final week, so it's fresh on the day
* Sessions spread across all six weeks, with growing gaps
- One long session now, and nothing until the exam itself
= Longer goals suit longer gaps, spread over the whole time.

Q: What decides when 4YOS shows you a flashcard again?
P: Making a schedule
- A fixed timetable that's exactly the same for every card
* Your own ratings, through the FSRS scheduler
- The order in which the cards were first created
= Each card's gap comes from how you rated it.

Q[transfer]: You're learning a new programming language for a job that starts in two months. Which plan fits this lesson?
P: Spacing with two schools
- Study it for twelve hours over one weekend just before you start
* Practise a little most days for the two months, recalling what you did before
- Read the whole language manual once, now, while you have the time
= Spaced, recall-based practice over the whole time lasts.

Q[transfer]: An assignment is due in three weeks. Which plan fits best?
P: Spacing with two schools
- Start it the night before, when the pressure helps you focus
* Do a small part on several days from now on
- Write it all in one long session in the second week
= Spread over days, each part gets more thought, and less of it is rushed.

Q[transfer]: Your friend rates every flashcard "Easy" to get through them faster. What will happen?
P: Making a schedule
* The cards come back after long gaps, often after they're forgotten
- The cards will be remembered better, because they come back less
- Nothing will happen, because ratings only affect the colour of the cards
= Generous ratings make the scheduler wait too long.
