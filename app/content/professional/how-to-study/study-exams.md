# Goal
Use a permitted past or specimen question to rehearse under relevant exam conditions, diagnose lost marks, and plan a targeted retry.

# Why
Recognising the topic in a past paper can make you feel ready. An exam also asks you to interpret instructions, use your time and show enough of the method to earn the available credit. A short rehearsal using your actual course can reveal which part you need to practise before the assessment.

# Warm-up
?? What are the four steps of a recall attempt?
=> Close the source, recall, check, then correct only what you missed or got wrong.

?? Why mix question types in a practice session?
=> So that you practise choosing the method, as an exam makes you do.

# Lesson
## Find out what the current assessment expects
Recall the weekly planning rule from this course. Start with an obligation you can verify. For an exam, the authority is the latest instruction from the relevant school, course team or exam notice. Record which course it applies to and when you checked it. Write down the current duration, question choice, weighting, approved materials and answer format. An open-book assessment still has rules about resources and collaboration. If an exam format or allowed tool is uncertain, leave it as a question and check the official channel.

A past paper can show you how questions have been worded and how a previous paper was organised. A specimen paper can show an example structure. An older paper cannot establish that a current exam still has that structure or will ask the same topics. Use authorised releases, course-provided practice, or questions from your own course materials that you may use. Leave current restricted papers and files of uncertain origin alone. If no past paper is available, you can still practise with an authorised worksheet question and record what you cannot verify about the format.

## Read the task before choosing a method
Underline the action and the limits of the question. An instruction to state an answer may require only the requested value or fact. Explain usually asks you to give the reason or mechanism. Compare asks you to consider both items on a common basis. Interpret asks you to connect a result to what it means in context. Check your course's guidance for how those words are marked. If the question gives assumptions or a unit, carry them into your answer.

For a statistics prompt that asks you to calculate a proportion and interpret it, show which count is the numerator and which group is the denominator. Then say in words what the result is a proportion of. The number alone leaves the interpretation unfinished. In a computing or security prompt asking why a control is useful, first identify the specific problem the control addresses. Explain how it changes the situation and include any boundary named in the question. A list of definitions is only useful if it answers the task.

## Give questions a time budget
Set aside time to read the instructions, choose any optional questions and check your work. Divide the remaining writing time across the marks you are expected to attempt. If a paper allows 75 minutes, you reserve 5 to read and 10 to check, and the required answers total 60 marks, that leaves 60 minutes of writing. A 12-mark question starts with about 12 minutes and an 18-mark question with about 18. This is a guide. Adjust for sections that require setup, reading or calculations. Avoid allocating time to every question on the page when you are only required to answer some of them.

For practice, record a stop time and try a short set before attempting a whole paper. Match the resources and delivery format as far as you can verify. A timed calculation on paper and a typed response in a portal may call for different checks. When a question is taking too long, put down useful partial working where allowed, mark it for review, and move on. When you return, check unanswered items, units, question numbers and submission instructions before polishing the wording.

## Learn from the evidence you produced
Keep the answer exactly as it was at the stop time. Compare it with an official mark scheme or worked solution if one is available. Look for what actually earned credit, such as a method step, a supported conclusion or a correct interpretation. If you have only notes, record what you could verify and what is still uncertain. Do not assign yourself an official grade. Ask a course tutor about an ambiguous instruction or answer if you cannot resolve it.

Sort the missing parts into practical categories. A knowledge gap needs a brief review followed by a closed-source explanation. A misread task needs a new question with the same action word. A calculation error needs a clean run with the working visible. A timing problem needs a smaller timed set with a stopping rule. Preserve the original attempt, note the correction separately and choose a fresh question for a later retry. That second attempt gives you evidence that you can apply the correction beyond the question you already saw.

> Mistake: Reading the model answer first and then timing yourself on that same question as if it were new. Use that round to study the worked answer, then use a fresh question for your rehearsal.

# Exercises
?? A paper gives 90 minutes, including 5 minutes to read and 10 minutes to check. The required answers total 50 marks. What starting time budget fits a 10-mark question?
=> There are 75 minutes to write. Divide 75 by 50 to get 1.5 minutes per mark, then multiply by 10. Start with 15 minutes and adjust for the paper's sections.

?? You gave an accurate number, but the question also asked what it meant for the population named in the question. Which part needs work?
=> Add the interpretation tied to that population. Keep the original answer as evidence that the calculation and the interpretation are separate skills.

?? An older paper asks for four answers, but the current verified instruction asks for three. Which rule belongs in your next mock?
=> Use the current instruction. Practise relevant questions from the old paper within the current limits, or label the attempt as a different format.

# Practice
Choose one real course you're taking. Use a question from its authorised practice materials and find the latest assessment instructions available to you.

1. Record the course, source and date checked. Note the current rules you can confirm and one point to verify if anything is unclear.
2. Select a small set from the authorised material. Write the action each prompt asks for and allocate reading, writing and checking time. Leave the answer key out of sight.
3. Run the rehearsal using the permitted resources. Stop at the planned time and preserve your original answer.
4. Review it with the published marking guide or source material. Label one issue by type and choose a fresh question to attempt later. If a score cannot be verified, record the issue without a score.

Save your practice record with the date, time budget, original outcome and retry plan. Do not copy restricted assessment content into 4YOS.

# Rubric
- I used an authorised question and separated current rules from older paper instructions.
- I used a time budget and preserved my first answer before checking.
- I identified an issue from my own attempt and chose a fresh follow-up.

# Summary
- Verify current rules with the school before borrowing a format from a past paper.
- Match the action in the question and show the reasoning it calls for.
- Reserve time to read and check, then budget writing by required marks.
- Keep the first attempt and use its specific gaps to plan a fresh retry.
