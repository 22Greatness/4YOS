# Goal
Distinguish familiarity from what you can explain without notes, then measure the gap on real course material and use it to decide what to study next.

# Why
Time spent reading can feel productive even when the ideas disappear as soon as you close the page. A small check early in a study session can tell you what to practise while there is still time to fix it. You can use the same method on a lecture, a course reading, or a lesson here.

# Lesson
## Two ways of knowing a page
Before reading on, think of the last page you studied. With the page closed, name its main idea and one reason or example that supports it. If you can remember the page's appearance or the location of a heading more easily than its argument, you have already seen the difference this lesson is about.

**Recognition** means you can identify something when it appears in front of you. **Recall** means you can bring it to mind when the source is out of sight. You may recognise an explanation immediately when you see it, yet be unable to give the explanation in your own words. A heading, a highlighted phrase, and the previous sentence all supply cues while you are reading. Close the page and those cues disappear.

**Familiarity** is the feeling that you have encountered something before. It is useful for finding where to look, but that feeling alone cannot tell you which parts you could explain without help. **Calibration** means comparing how well you expect to do with how well you actually do on a defined task. A useful prediction sounds like “I expect to recall three of these four ideas,” because you can check it against a result.

## What happened when students put the page away
Roediger and Karpicke studied students learning short prose passages. After the first reading, some students read the passages again. Others practised producing the contents from memory without seeing the answers. Different groups returned for a final recall test after five minutes, two days, or one week. The useful comparison is what the groups could remember at the same delay.

At five minutes, repeated study gave better recall. At the longer delays, the students who had practised recall remembered more. Repeated studying also increased students' confidence about remembering later. The same activity could therefore raise confidence today while doing less for the result the students cared about several days later. The outcome depended on when memory was checked.

The study used prose passages and a recall test. A programming task, a calculation, or a security investigation needs practice doing that kind of work too. For example, after learning how to read file permissions, explain the meaning of a rule from memory and then inspect a file you own. Each action answers a different question about what you can do.

## Make a prediction you can check
Set a small target before you close the source. Pick four specific points, such as a definition, a difference, a reason, and an example. A section heading is a useful prompt, provided you keep the actual answer covered. Write down how many points you expect to get, from zero to four. Then put the material away and write your answers without checking as you go.

### Worked example: checking an explanation about computer files
Use these four targets for a short passage on backups: what a backup is, why it helps when the original is lost, why a shortcut is insufficient, and how to verify that a backup can be used.

1. Predict 4 out of 4 while the passage still feels fresh.
2. Close it. Write that a backup is a separate copy, that you can recover a lost original from it, and that a shortcut points to the original. Leave the fourth point blank if it does not come to mind.
3. Reopen the passage and check each point. If those first three explanations are accurate, the closed-book result is 3 out of 4. The gap between prediction and performance is one point.
4. Revisit the missing idea: verify that a copy can be opened or restored. Close the passage again and explain that action in your own words. The first score remains 3 out of 4. Record the correction separately.

A score based on meaning works better here than copying the original wording. Give a point only when the central idea is accurate. If an answer is partly right or vague, write what was missing. The purpose is to decide what needs practice, so count guesses, blanks and errors honestly.

## Decide what to do with the gap
A missing point tells you where to reopen the source. Read that small piece, fix your explanation, and try it again with the answer out of view. If you go completely blank, use a smaller passage or give yourself a short cue such as the heading. Record whether you needed that cue. When the task is manageable, try it again without the cue.

Rereading still has useful jobs. Use it to understand an idea for the first time, repair a specific mistake, or confirm a detail. After that, put the material away and see what remains. A strong score right after reading is good information about that moment. Schedule another short attempt tomorrow or later in the week if you want evidence that it lasted.

> Mistake: Looking at the answer and changing a blank into a correct mark because it now feels obvious. Keep the original result and write the correction next to it.

# Exercises
?? You predict four correct points and produce two accurate points, one vague point, and one blank. What belongs in your first score and what do you do next?
=> Score 2 out of 4. Mark what the vague response lacked and why the blank matters, then reread those two pieces and retry them with the source closed.

?? A friend can identify the right definition from a list but cannot explain it from a heading alone. What has the friend demonstrated?
=> Recognition with the available choices. The next useful check is to produce the definition or an example with those choices hidden.

?? You answer everything right after reading a page. How could you find out whether the same knowledge is available on a later day?
=> Put a fresh closed-source attempt on a later day. Compare the delayed result with the immediate result using the same scoring targets.

# Practice
Choose one real page from a course reading, or use a completed 4YOS lesson you want to keep. Use a passage short enough to read in about four minutes.

1. Write four prompts tied to the passage. Pick one main idea, one difference or relation, one reason, and one concrete example. Keep the answers separate.
2. Predict how many of the four you will answer accurately. Put the source and answer cues out of sight, then write your answers. Spend up to four minutes.
3. Check against the source and record your original score. Write one correction for each point you missed. If you got all four, add a new example prompt and check it the same way.
4. Reattempt a missed point after closing the source. Set a date for a brief delayed attempt on the same four targets, with prompts but no answers in view.

Record the actual topic, prediction, original score, one correction or new example, and the delayed attempt date. Keep private course material and personal identifiers in your own notes.

# Rubric
- I used real material and recorded a prediction before attempting four prompts with the source closed.
- I kept the original score separate from any answers I corrected after looking.
- I recorded a specific correction or new example and set a date to check my recall again.

# Summary
- Recognition happens with cues present. Recall means producing the idea yourself.
- Compare a prediction with a closed-source score to calibrate your confidence.
- Reread a specific gap, then test the corrected idea with the source out of view.
- Check again after a delay if your goal is remembering beyond today's session.
