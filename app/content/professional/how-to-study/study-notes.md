# Goal
Take notes that make you think while you write them, and that turn into recall practice later: in your own words, with questions in the margin, and a short summary.

# Why
Notes can be a record you never look at, or a study tool you use for months. The difference is what you do while taking them and afterwards. Copying a slide word for word keeps your hand busy and your mind quiet. Notes in your own words, with questions to test yourself, make the lecture the first step of your recall practice instead of the last time you think about it.

# Warm-up
?? What makes a good recall question?
=> It asks why, how, what if or what's the difference, so you explain or use the idea.

?? In your own words, what does it mean to self-explain a worked example?
=> Saying what each step does and why it's allowed.

# Lesson
## Copying against processing
There are two ways to take notes. **Transcribing** means writing down what's said or shown, as completely as possible. **Generative note-taking** means putting ideas into your own words: summarising, picking out what matters, linking ideas and noting questions.

| Transcribing | Generative |
| --- | --- |
| Copies the slide word for word | Writes the main point in your own words |
| Tries to get everything | Chooses what matters |
| Little thinking during the lecture | Thinking about meaning during the lecture |
| Produces long notes you won't reread | Produces shorter notes you can use |

Generative notes help because choosing and rewording forces you to understand. You can't put an idea in your own words without thinking about what it means.

### Laptop or paper?
A widely reported 2014 study found that students who took notes on paper understood more than those using laptops, possibly because laptop users typed lectures word for word. Later, larger studies that tried to repeat it found the difference much smaller or unclear. A direct replication found no immediate learning advantage for handwriting. Both formats can record useful notes. So don't worry about the tool: summarise the main idea in your own words, then use a closed-note check to see what you can really explain. If a laptop tempts you to transcribe or to open other tabs, paper may suit you better. If not, type, but in your own words.

## A page that becomes a test
The **Cornell method** divides each page into three areas. It's useful because it builds recall practice into the notes themselves.

| Area | Where | What goes there | When |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Notes | Right, the largest part | Main ideas in your own words | During the lecture or reading |
| Cues | Left margin | Questions about the notes | Within a day, afterwards |
| Summary | Bottom | Two or three sentences on the whole page | Within a day, afterwards |

The cue column turns the notes into a test. Later, cover the notes area, read each question on the left, and answer from memory. Then uncover and check. That's the recall cycle, built into the page.

### Worked example: a statistics lecture
| Cues | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| Why does the median resist outliers? | Median = middle value when sorted. One huge value doesn't move it much, unlike the mean |
| When would the mean mislead? | Incomes in a street with one very rich person: the mean is high, but most earn far less |
| What do I report for skewed data? | Median, often with the range of the middle half |

Summary: For data with extreme values, the median describes a typical value better than the mean, because the median doesn't depend on the extreme values themselves.

You don't have to draw lines exactly. What matters is having the notes, the questions and the summary, and using the questions later.

## During and after
### During a lecture
- Write the **main ideas** and **examples**, not every word. If the slides will be shared, don't copy them, and note what the lecturer says that isn't on them.
- Leave **space**, so you can add things later.
- Mark what you don't understand with a **?** to sort out afterwards.
- Write formulas and worked steps carefully, since those are hard to rebuild later.

### After the lecture, within a day
This is where notes pay off, and most people skip it.

1. **Fill gaps** while you still remember: tidy unclear parts, resolve your ? marks, using the slides or a classmate.
2. **Write questions** in the cue column, using why, how and what if.
3. **Write the summary** at the bottom, with the notes covered if you can.
4. **Test yourself** once using the cues.

Then put the cue questions into your spaced review. They come back in a few days, then a week later, like flashcards.

> Mistake: Rewriting messy notes neatly as "revision". It feels productive and looks beautiful, but it's copying. Spend that time answering your cue questions instead.

## Notes from reading
For course materials and textbooks, the same ideas apply, with one change: read a section first, then close it and write notes from memory. This is recall while note-taking. Then reopen it, check, and add only what you missed.

Turning each heading into a question first gives you a reason to read and a cue for later. A heading such as "Types of malware" becomes "What types of malware are there, and how does each spread?".

# Exercises
?? Rewrite this transcribed note in your own words: "A variable is a named location in memory that stores a value which may change during program execution."
=> For example: "A variable is a name for a value the program keeps, which can change while it runs."

?? Write two cue questions for a note about spacing.
=> For example: "Why does spaced study beat cramming?" and "What should I do first when I come back to a topic?"

?? The slides will be shared after the lecture. What should your notes focus on?
=> What the lecturer says that isn't on the slides, such as examples, explanations and warnings, in your own words.

# Practice
Use your next real lecture or reading at either school.

1. Take notes in your own words, with a margin left for cues and space at the bottom for a summary.
2. Within a day, fill the gaps, write at least four cue questions, and write a two- or three-sentence summary.
3. Cover the notes, and answer each cue from memory. Then check.
4. Add the cue questions to your review dates for the next week.

Write your summary, your cue questions and how many you answered correctly from memory.

# Rubric
- My notes are in my own words, not copied from the slides.
- I added cue questions and a summary within a day.
- I tested myself using the cues, and scheduled them for review.

# Summary
- Copying word for word keeps your mind quiet. Your own words make you think.
- The tool matters less than processing the ideas.
- Cornell notes: notes, cue questions in the margin, and a summary.
- Within a day: fill gaps, write cues, summarise, test yourself.
- From readings: read, close, write from memory, then check.

# Check
Q: What is generative note-taking?
P: Copying against processing
- Writing down everything the lecturer says, word for word
* Putting the ideas into your own words, choosing what matters
- Taking a clear photo of each slide on your phone, to read again later at home
= Choosing and rewording forces you to think about meaning.

Q: What did later research find about the famous laptop-versus-paper study?
P: Copying against processing
* The difference was smaller or unclear, and processing matters more than the tool
- Paper was proven to be about twice as good as laptops in every study, for every kind of course
- Laptops were found to be better than paper in every situation tested
= The lasting lesson is that word-for-word copying is weak, whatever the tool.

Q: In Cornell notes, what goes in the left margin?
P: A page that becomes a test
- A copy of the lecturer's slides, made smaller
* Questions about the notes, for testing yourself later
- The date, the course code, the venue and the lecturer's name, for your records
= The cue questions turn the page into recall practice.

Q: How do you use Cornell notes to revise?
P: A page that becomes a test
- Reread the notes column carefully, then the summary at the bottom, several times over
* Cover the notes, answer the cue questions, then check
- Copy the notes out again, more neatly than before
= It's the recall cycle, using your own questions.

Q: The lecturer's slides will be shared. What should you write down?
P: During and after
- Every bullet point on every slide, word for word
* What's said that isn't on the slides, in your own words
- Nothing at all, because the shared slides will contain everything that matters
= The examples and explanations are what the slides leave out.

Q: When should you add cue questions and a summary?
P: During and after
* Within a day, while you still remember the lecture
- During the exam week, when you start revising and have more time to spare
- Never, because they take time away from studying
= Afterwards, while it's fresh, is when gaps are easiest to fill.

Q: Is rewriting messy notes neatly good revision?
P: During and after
- Yes, because neat notes are much easier to learn from
* No, it's copying, so answer your cue questions instead
- Yes, as long as you use several colours of pen
= Rewriting feels productive, but it's not recall.

Q: How should you take notes from a reading?
P: Notes from reading
- Copy each important sentence while reading it
* Read, close it, write from memory, then check
- Highlight the important parts, and write nothing down
= Writing from memory makes note-taking recall practice.

Q[transfer]: In a security course, you hear about three types of malware. Which note works best?
P: Copying against processing
* Each type in your own words, with how it spreads, plus a question to test yourself
- The lecturer's exact sentences about each type, word for word
- Just the three names, to look up later if needed
= Your own words plus a cue question make it usable for recall.

Q[transfer]: In a work meeting, you'll need to act on what's decided. Which note fits this lesson?
P: During and after
- Every word said by everyone in the meeting
* Decisions and actions in your own words, checked and tidied the same day
- No notes, since you'll remember the important parts
= Processing and a same-day check work beyond school too.

Q[transfer]: A course unit has headings such as "Causes of inflation". How can you use them?
P: Notes from reading
- Copy each heading into your notes as a title
* Turn each heading into a question before reading, and answer it afterwards
- Skip the headings, and read only the main text
= Questions from headings give you a reason to read and a cue for later.
