# Goal
Protect the things your learning depends on: sleep, focus, breaks, movement and asking for help. Plan a pace you can keep for four years, not one you can keep for a week.

# Why
Two degrees is a long effort, and the biggest risk isn't one bad exam. It's running yourself down until you can't learn at all. Memory needs sleep. Focus needs breaks. Motivation needs rest and progress you can see. This lesson is about studying in a way your body and mind can keep up with, because how well you are affects how well you learn.

# Lesson
## Sleep is part of studying
While you sleep, your brain processes and strengthens what you learned during the day. This is called **consolidation**. Research consistently finds that sleep after learning helps memory, and that missing sleep makes it harder both to learn new things and to concentrate.

That makes an all-night cram a poor trade. You gain a few hours of tired study and lose the sleep that would have helped keep what you studied. You also go into the exam with less ability to think clearly.

| Choice the night before an exam | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Study until 4 a.m. | A few extra hours at low quality, little consolidation, a slow mind in the exam |
| Light recall, then sleep | What you learned earlier is strengthened, and you think more clearly |

Health guidance generally recommends that adults get at least seven hours of sleep a night. You may not manage that every night with two schools, but treat sleep as a fixed commitment in your weekly plan, not as the first thing you cut.

## Focus in short blocks
Attention fades during long sessions, and it's broken by interruptions. Every notification you check costs more than the moment it takes, because it takes time to get back into deep focus.

- **Work in blocks** of about 25 to 50 minutes, then take a short break of 5 to 10 minutes. The exact length matters less than having a clear start and stop.
- **Put your phone away**, in another room or face down and silent. Having it in reach pulls at your attention, even when you don't check it.
- **Decide the task before you start**: "15 recall questions on Unit 3", not "study".
- **Take real breaks**: stand up, move, drink water, look away from screens. Scrolling isn't a rest for your attention.

This fits what you know about spacing: several focused blocks spread across a day beat one long, distracted sitting.

## A pace you can keep
Feeling tired and behind sometimes is normal. A pattern of feeling exhausted, unable to concentrate, and dreading study for weeks is a warning sign of **burnout**, and pushing harder usually makes it worse.

Things that help keep a sustainable pace:

- **Rest on purpose.** Plan at least some time each week with no study at all. Rest is part of the plan, not a failure of it.
- **Move your body.** Regular physical activity, even walking, is linked to better mood and better concentration.
- **Eat and drink regularly.** Long study sessions without food or water make focus harder.
- **Notice progress.** Your progress log and Library show what you've really done. On a hard week, look at how far you've come.
- **Cut, don't crash.** When a week is too full, use the weekly review to drop or move things on purpose, before you run out.

4YOS is built to support this. If you tell Ayo you're tired, Today becomes 15 minutes or rest, and nothing is lost: the plan moves.

> Mistake: Treating rest as wasted time and guilt as motivation. Guilt drains energy, and steady progress needs energy. After a rest, check whether you can focus and answer a few questions, and set the next block to what you can sustain.

## Asking for help
Sometimes the problem isn't study technique. Money worries, family pressure, illness, or low mood can make study impossible, and those are reasons to get support, not reasons to blame yourself.

- **Talk to someone you trust**: a friend, a family member, someone at your place of worship.
- **Use your schools' support.** Universities have student affairs and counselling services. Find out what your school offers before you need it.
- **Tell your school early** if illness or a crisis is affecting your studies. Many schools have procedures for this, but usually only if you speak up in time.
- **See a health professional** for health concerns, including persistent low mood, poor sleep or anxiety.

If you ever feel unable to keep yourself safe, contact emergency services or go to the nearest hospital straight away, or reach someone near you who can help.

> Note: 4YOS and Ayo are study tools. They can't replace a doctor, a counsellor or a real person who cares about you.

# Exercises
?? It's the night before an exam, and you're tired. What's the better plan?
=> Light recall on the main points, then a full night's sleep, rather than studying through the night.

?? Rewrite "study for 4 hours" as focused work.
=> For example: four 45-minute blocks on specific tasks, with breaks between them and the phone in another room.

?? A friend has felt exhausted and unable to concentrate for a month, and keeps pushing harder. What would you suggest?
=> Rest on purpose, cut the load using a weekly review, and talk to someone, such as the school's counselling service or a doctor.

# Practice
Look after the basics for one real week.

1. Put sleep into your weekly plan as a fixed commitment, and record how many hours you actually sleep each night.
2. Do every study session as focused blocks, with the phone out of reach, and note how many blocks you complete.
3. Plan at least one block of rest, with no study at all, and keep it.
4. Find out what student support services your school offers, and where they are, and write it down.
5. At the weekly review, compare your sleep and your recall scores.

Write your average sleep, your number of focused blocks, and where your schools' support services are. Keep private details out.

# Rubric
- I treated sleep as a fixed commitment and recorded it honestly.
- I studied in focused blocks with my phone away, and planned real rest.
- I found out where my schools' support services are.

# Summary
- Sleep consolidates learning. An all-nighter trades memory for tired hours.
- Study in focused blocks with real breaks, and keep your phone out of reach.
- Plan rest on purpose, move, eat, and notice your progress.
- Weeks of exhaustion and dread are a warning sign: cut the load and get support.
- Ask for help early, from people, your schools and health professionals.
