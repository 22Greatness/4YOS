# Goal
Explain to yourself why each idea or step is true and how it links to what you already know, and use that explaining to find gaps before an exam finds them.

# Why
Knowing the steps of a method isn't the same as knowing why they work. Without the why, a small change in the question breaks you. Explaining connects a new idea to what you already know, which makes it easier to recall and easier to use in a new situation. It's also the skill a lecturer is testing when a question says "explain" or "justify".

# Warm-up
?? What are the four steps of the recall cycle?
=> Close the source, recall, check, then correct what you missed.

?? Which makes a stronger recall question: "What does RAM stand for?" or "Why is unsaved work lost when the power goes?"
=> The second, because it asks you to explain and use the idea.

# Lesson
## Asking why
**Elaboration** means adding meaning to a fact by asking how and why, and connecting it to things you already know. One form is **elaborative interrogation**: for each fact, ask "why would this be true?" and try to answer.

| Fact | A why-question | An answer that connects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RAM loses its contents without power | Why? | It holds bits as tiny electrical charges, which need power to stay |
| Spacing beats cramming | Why? | Recalling after some forgetting takes effort, and effort strengthens memory |
| The median resists outliers | Why? | It depends only on the middle value, so one extreme value can't drag it |

Try to answer before you look it up. If you can't, that's useful: you've found what to study. Then check your answer against the source. A wrong explanation that you believe is worse than none, so the check matters here as much as in recall.

## Explaining each step
**Self-explanation** means saying, out loud or in writing, what each step of a worked example does and why it's there. Studies of students learning from worked examples found that those who explained the steps to themselves understood more and solved new problems better than those who just read the steps.

### Worked example: self-explaining a solution
The textbook shows: $3x + 5 = 20$, so $3x = 15$, so $x = 5$.

| Step | Self-explanation |
| --- | --- |
| $3x + 5 = 20$ to $3x = 15$ | I subtracted 5 from both sides, to undo the $+5$ and keep the balance |
| $3x = 15$ to $x = 5$ | I divided both sides by 3, to undo the multiplication by 3 |
| Check | $3 \times 5 + 5 = 20$, so it's right |

Each line answers "what did I do, and why was that allowed?" If you can't say why a step was allowed, you've found exactly where your understanding has a hole.

### Explain it to someone who doesn't know
A useful test is to explain the idea in simple words, as if to a friend who hasn't studied it, or to an empty chair. Where you reach for jargon, or say "it just does", you've found a gap. Go back to the source for that part, then explain again.

> Mistake: Explaining with the source open and reading it back. That's rereading in disguise. Close the source first, explain, then check.

## Making it concrete
Abstract ideas become easier to understand and remember when you tie them to **concrete examples**. For every idea, find or make an example, and then a second one that looks different, so you see what they share.

| Idea | Example 1 | Example 2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Percentage increase | A ₦2,000 fee rises to ₦2,400: up 20% | A class of 50 grows to 60: up 20% |
| Confidentiality | Your exam results seen by a stranger | A company's customer list leaked online |
| Compound growth | Debt that grows on its interest | A rumour where each person tells two more |

Two different examples stop you tying the idea to one surface detail. If your only example of a percentage is about money, you may not recognise a percentage question about people.

### Linking to what you know
Ask how a new idea relates to ideas you already have. Is it like something? Different from something? A special case of something? For example, ratio is like fractions, interest is percentages used repeatedly, and a byte is place value with only two digits. Each link gives the memory another path back.

## Using this with Ayo
In each lesson, try explaining a part yourself before asking Ayo. Ayo's explanation can help you check, or unstick you, but the learning happens in your own attempt to explain. Asking first skips the most useful step.

When Ayo or a book explains something, explain it back in your own words afterwards, with the page closed. If you can't, you followed the explanation without understanding it.

# Exercises
?? Write a why-question and an answer for: "Dividing by a fraction below 1 gives a bigger answer."
=> Why? Because you're asking how many small pieces fit into the number, and many small pieces fit. For example, $6 \div \frac{1}{2} = 12$.

?? Self-explain this step: "$2(x + 3) = 14$ becomes $2x + 6 = 14$."
=> I multiplied out the bracket: the 2 multiplies both the $x$ and the 3, by the distributive law.

?? Give two different examples of "an average can mislead".
=> For example: the mean income of a street with one billionaire, and the mean score of a class where half got 0 and half got 100.

# Practice
Use one real worked example or explanation from a course you're taking now.

1. Close the source and explain the idea in simple words, out loud or in writing, as if to someone who hasn't studied it. Mark every place you got stuck or used a word you couldn't explain.
2. Open the source and fix those places only.
3. Take one worked example from the topic, and write a self-explanation for each step: what you did and why it was allowed.
4. Write two concrete examples of the main idea that look different from each other.

Write your stuck points, your step-by-step explanation and your two examples.

# Rubric
- I explained the idea with the source closed, and marked where I got stuck.
- I wrote what and why for every step of a worked example.
- I gave two different concrete examples of the idea.

# Summary
- Ask "why would this be true?" for each fact, and answer before checking.
- Self-explain each step of a worked example: what you did and why it was allowed.
- Explaining in simple words shows you where the gaps are.
- Tie each idea to two concrete examples that look different.
- Try to explain before asking Ayo. The attempt is where the learning happens.

# Check
Q: What is elaborative interrogation?
P: Asking why
- Reading a passage several times until it feels familiar
* Asking why each fact is true, and trying to answer it
- Writing every fact out in full sentences, neatly
= It adds meaning by connecting a fact to its reasons.

Q: You can't answer your own why-question. What does that tell you?
P: Asking why
* You've found exactly what to study next
- The method isn't working for this subject at all
- The fact isn't important enough to learn
= A gap you've found is a gap you can fix.

Q: What is self-explanation?
P: Explaining each step
- Reading a worked example's steps silently, in order
* Saying what each step does and why it's allowed
- Copying a worked example out until it's memorised
= Explaining each step shows whether you understand it.

Q: Self-explaining, you can't say why a step was allowed. What should you do?
P: Explaining each step
- Move on, since the final answer in the book is correct
* Go back to the source for that step, then explain it again
- Memorise the step exactly as it's written in the book
= That's where your understanding has a hole.

Q: You explain an idea while reading it straight from the textbook. What's wrong?
P: Explaining each step
- Nothing, because the textbook's explanation is the correct one
* It's rereading in disguise, so close the source first
- You should be explaining it from a different textbook
= Explaining only tests you with the source out of sight.

Q: Why use two concrete examples that look different?
P: Making it concrete
- Two examples make your notes look much more complete
* So you see what they share, not just one surface detail
- Because exams always ask for exactly two examples
= Different surfaces show the idea underneath.

Q: How does linking a new idea to one you know help?
P: Making it concrete
* It gives the memory another path back
- It replaces the need to understand the new idea
- It means you can skip recall practice for it
= Each connection is another way to find the memory.

Q: When should you ask Ayo to explain a part of a lesson?
P: Using this with Ayo
- Straight away, before thinking about it yourself
* After you've tried to explain it yourself first
- Never, because asking for help stops learning
= Your own attempt is the learning. Ayo helps you check or get unstuck.

Q[transfer]: You must learn what a firewall does. Which gives the best understanding?
P: Asking why
- Memorising a one-line definition of a firewall word for word
* Explaining why a firewall blocks some traffic and what problem that solves
- Reading three definitions of a firewall from three websites
= Explaining the why connects it to the problem it solves.

Q[transfer]: In statistics, you followed a worked example on the median. How can you check you understood it?
P: Explaining each step
* Close it, then explain each step and why it's done
- Read the worked example once more, slowly
- Highlight the final answer in the worked example
= Explaining each step from memory tests real understanding.

Q[transfer]: A friend asks you to explain compound interest, and you say "it just grows faster". What does that suggest?
P: Explaining each step
- You understand it well, since a short answer is best
* You've found a gap, as you can't yet explain why it grows faster
- Your friend asked a question that doesn't have an answer
= "It just does" marks the spot to go back and study.
